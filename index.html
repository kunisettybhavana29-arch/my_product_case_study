Loan Management System: a business analysis case study

I took a fictional lender that runs loans on spreadsheets and email, worked out what was going wrong, and designed the system that should replace it, from the first interview question to the last UAT test case.

By Bhavana · Business analysis portfolio · Client: Meridian Finance (fictional retail NBFC)

Meridian is invented, and so are the numbers. Baselines and targets are assumptions I set to make the analysis concrete. The dashboard and SQL results come from a sample database I generated (420 applications, 334 loans), not real borrowers.

The problem

Meridian lends through branches and agents (DSAs). Applications are keyed in twice, KYC is checked by hand, approvals travel by email, and collections run off a spreadsheet. The result, in my baseline: loans take about 9 working days to disburse, 38% of applicants drop off before a decision, 14% of files need rework, and the portfolio at risk (PAR 30) sits at 11.5%.

Requirements

The full BRD (15 business requirements, prioritised with MoSCoW), FRD (19 functional requirements, 7 business rules, 6 non-functional requirements), 10 user stories with Given/When/Then criteria and a traceability matrix are in the written document: read the full BRD and FRD.

Three business rules that shaped the design

FOIR (existing plus new EMIs as a share of net income) cannot exceed 50%. Loans up to ₹3 lakh with a score of 750+ and FOIR of 40% or less can be approved without a human. Anything above ₹5 lakh goes to a senior underwriter, and above ₹15 lakh to a credit committee.

One story, in full

US-05: Approve, reject or refer back. As an underwriter, I want to record a decision with a reason so decisions are consistent and auditable.
  • Given a loan within my limit, when I approve, then the status becomes Approved and a sanction letter is generated.
  • Given a loan above my limit, then it routes to the next approver automatically.
  • Given I reject or refer back without a reason code, then the system blocks the action.

Process: before and after

As-is
  1. Customer fills a paper form; the branch re-types it into Excel
  2. Back office checks KYC by hand (1–2 days)
  3. Analyst pulls the bureau report and works out FOIR in a sheet
  4. Approver reviews an email chain (2–3 days)
  5. Finance disburses on an emailed OK, with no second check
To-be
  1. One digital application, validated as it is typed
  2. e-KYC, bureau pull and FOIR run automatically
  3. Rules engine approves, rejects or routes exceptions
  4. Underwriter decides on a single screen; customer e-signs
  5. Disbursement needs a maker and a different checker

SQL analysis

I built the schema in SQLite (five tables) and loaded simulated data so every query below actually runs. The sample data is described at the bottom of the page. The as-of date for overdue calculations is 31 Oct 2026.

Where do applications get lost? Conversion by channel

SELECT channel, COUNT(*) AS applications,
       SUM(status = 'Disbursed') AS disbursed,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(status = 'Disbursed') / COUNT(*), 1) AS conversion_pct
FROM loan_applications
GROUP BY channel ORDER BY conversion_pct DESC;

What I’d take from this: agent-sourced applications convert about 8 points lower than branch ones, which points to weaker pre-screening. A checklist at the point of capture (FR-01 and FR-05) is the first thing I’d test.

Is the monthly book growing? (LAG)

WITH monthly AS (
  SELECT strftime('%Y-%m', disbursal_date) AS month, SUM(disbursed_amount) AS disbursed
  FROM loans GROUP BY 1)
SELECT month, disbursed,
       ROUND(100.0 * (disbursed - LAG(disbursed) OVER (ORDER BY month))
             / LAG(disbursed) OVER (ORDER BY month), 1) AS mom_growth_pct
FROM monthly;

Disbursement swings between ₹1.3 Cr (Jun) and ₹2.7 Cr (Feb) a month, with July up 73% on June. Large month-to-month swings like that are why I put the MoM measure on the executive page.

How much of the book is at risk? (PAR 30)

WITH overdue AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT loan_id FROM repayment_schedule
  WHERE paid_amount < emi_amount
    AND julianday('2026-10-31') - julianday(due_date) > 30)
SELECT ROUND(100.0 * SUM(CASE WHEN o.loan_id IS NOT NULL
                              THEN l.outstanding_principal END)
             / SUM(l.outstanding_principal), 2) AS par30_pct
FROM loans l LEFT JOIN overdue o ON o.loan_id = l.loan_id;

Result on the sample data: 7.98% of a ₹15.1 Cr book. Of the 36 loans with an unpaid EMI, 18 are already past 90 days. That tail is where I’d point the collections team first.

Collection efficiency by month

SELECT strftime('%Y-%m', due_date) AS month,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(paid_amount) / SUM(emi_amount), 1) AS collection_eff_pct
FROM repayment_schedule
WHERE due_date <= '2026-10-31'
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 1;

The DAX behind it

One correction I made while checking my work: approval rate should only count applications that have been decided, and PAR 30 needs a days-past-due value per loan, which I supply from a SQL view.

-- SQL view feeding FactLoans[MaxDPD]
CREATE VIEW vw_loan_dpd AS
SELECT loan_id,
       MAX(CASE WHEN paid_amount < emi_amount
                THEN julianday('now') - julianday(due_date) END) AS max_dpd
FROM repayment_schedule WHERE due_date < date('now') GROUP BY loan_id;
Approval Rate % =
DIVIDE(
  CALCULATE(COUNTROWS(FactApplications),
            FactApplications[Status] IN { "Approved", "Disbursed" }),
  CALCULATE(COUNTROWS(FactApplications),
            FactApplications[Status] <> "Submitted"))

Avg TAT (Days) =
AVERAGEX(
  FILTER(FactApplications, NOT ISBLANK(FactApplications[DisbursalDate])),
  DATEDIFF(FactApplications[ApplicationDate], FactApplications[DisbursalDate], DAY))

Collection Efficiency % =
DIVIDE(SUM(FactRepayments[PaidAmount]), SUM(FactRepayments[EmiAmount]))

PAR 30 % =
DIVIDE(
  CALCULATE(SUM(FactLoans[OutstandingPrincipal]), FactLoans[MaxDPD] > 30),
  SUM(FactLoans[OutstandingPrincipal]))

Disbursed MoM % =
VAR Curr = [Total Disbursed]
VAR Prev = CALCULATE([Total Disbursed], DATEADD(DimDate[Date], -1, MONTH))
RETURN DIVIDE(Curr - Prev, Prev)

The report has four pages (executive summary, origination and turnaround, portfolio quality, collections) with branch, product and channel slicers, and row-level security so a branch manager sees only their branch.
